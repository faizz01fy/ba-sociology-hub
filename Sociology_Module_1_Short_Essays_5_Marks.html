Question 1 (5 Marks): Explain the major forces that led to the emergence of sociology.
ചോദ്യം 1 (5 മാർക്ക്): സോഷ്യോളജിയുടെ ആവിർഭാവത്തിലേക്ക് നയിച്ച പ്രധാന ശക്തികൾ വിശദീകരിക്കുക.
Model Answer (Exact Attachment Text · 148 Words)

Sociology emerged as an independent discipline in nineteenth-century Europe in response to far-reaching social change and new intellectual ideas. Three major forces contributed to its development:

1. Industrial Revolution: The shift from agrarian production to factories and machine-based industry encouraged migration from villages to cities and rapid urbanisation. It changed family life and occupations and created new classes, especially industrial capitalists and wage-earning workers. Poverty, unemployment, slums, overcrowding and poor working conditions prompted systematic study of society.

2. French Revolution (1789): The Revolution challenged monarchy and feudal privilege. Its ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity raised questions about rights, citizenship, power and social order. The breakdown of traditional institutions led thinkers such as Auguste Comte to consider how society could be studied and order rebuilt.

3. Renaissance and Enlightenment: These intellectual movements encouraged humanism, reason, scientific thinking, observation and criticism of traditional authority. They promoted the idea that society could be understood through evidence and rational analysis rather than only through religious explanations.

Together, these social, political and intellectual changes created the need and the means to study society systematically. This helped sociology develop as a distinct social science.

Question 2 (5 Marks): Describe the main characteristics of sociology as a science.
ചോദ്യം 2 (5 മാർക്ക്): ഒരു ശാസ്ത്രമെന്ന നിലയിൽ സോഷ്യോളജിയുടെ പ്രധാന സവിശേഷതകൾ വിവരിക്കുക.
Model Answer (Exact Attachment Text · Robert Bierstedt's 5 Core Characteristics)

Robert Bierstedt described several characteristics that explain sociology’s scientific nature:

1. Independent and social science: Sociology has its own subject matter and methods. It studies human social life, relationships, groups and institutions.

2. Categorical, not normative: Sociology analyses social facts, values and norms; it aims to explain them rather than judge them as morally right or wrong.

3. Pure and relatively abstract: Its primary aim is to build knowledge. It studies abstract patterns such as social structure, social systems and relationships.

4. Generalising and general in scope: Sociology looks for patterns across social life instead of studying every event in isolation. It examines society broadly and relates different aspects of social life.

5. Empirical and rational: It uses evidence gathered through methods such as observation, historical analysis and comparison, and interprets that evidence through reason and theory. Experimentation is difficult to apply strictly because people have freedom, feelings and individual preferences.

Thus, sociology is scientific because it studies society systematically using evidence and reason, while adapting its methods to the complexity of human social life.

Essay 1 (15 Marks · Comprehensive Long Essay)

Topic: Major Social, Political, and Intellectual Forces Behind the Emergence of Sociology
Question: Explain the major social, political and intellectual forces that contributed to the emergence of sociology as an independent discipline.
ചോദ്യം: ഒരു സ്വതന്ത്ര ശാസ്ത്രശാഖ എന്ന നിലയിൽ സോഷ്യോളജിയുടെ ആവിർഭാവത്തിന് കാരണമായ പ്രധാന സാമൂഹിക, രാഷ്ട്രീയ, ബൗദ്ധിക ശക്തികൾ വിശദീകരിക്കുക.
Model Answer (Complete Calicut University Master Essay Standard)

Introduction

Sociology is the systematic study of society, social relationships, institutions and patterns of interaction. Although people have reflected on social life since ancient times, sociology emerged as a distinct discipline in nineteenth-century Europe. Its formation was closely connected with major changes in social organisation and new ways of thinking. The Industrial Revolution and urbanisation, the French Revolution, and the intellectual movements of the Renaissance and Enlightenment created conditions in which society could be studied scientifically.

1. Social forces: industrialisation and urbanisation

The Industrial Revolution began in Britain in the eighteenth century and transformed an agrarian, craft-based economy into one organised around machines, factories and large-scale production. Work moved from households and small workshops to factories. Industries concentrated in towns and cities, drawing people from rural areas in search of employment. This process of urbanisation and migration altered population patterns, occupations, family life and community relationships.

Industrial society also brought new class divisions. Industrial capitalists owned factories and other means of production, while workers depended on wages. A growing middle class of professionals and administrators also became prominent. Karl Marx analysed the antagonism between the bourgeoisie and proletariat and the exploitation associated with capitalism. Max Weber examined class alongside status and power, while Durkheim considered how the division of labour created new forms of social differentiation and interdependence.

Rapid change generated problems that demanded systematic explanation: overcrowding, slums, poverty, unemployment, poor working conditions and crime. The weakening of traditional community ties and changes from extended to nuclear family patterns also drew attention. Marx, Engels, Durkheim and Weber studied the consequences of industrialisation in different ways. The need to understand social order, conflict and change helped establish sociology as an independent field.

2. Political forces: the French Revolution

The French Revolution of 1789 challenged the absolute monarchy and the feudal order. Before the Revolution, French society was divided into three estates: the clergy, the nobility and the Third Estate, comprising the majority, including peasants, workers and the bourgeoisie. The first two estates held privileges, while the Third Estate bore much of the tax burden and had limited political power. Inequality and dissatisfaction contributed to demands for rights and representation.

The Revolution weakened the authority of the monarchy, aristocracy and Church, and disrupted established social relationships. Its ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity, together with individual rights, democracy and secularism, shaped modern political thought. These ideas raised new questions about citizenship, authority, social order and the relationship between individuals and society. The upheaval also produced instability, encouraging thinkers such as Saint-Simon and Auguste Comte to ask how social order could be understood and reconstructed.

Early sociologists interpreted the Revolution differently. Comte saw the breakdown of traditional institutions as a period of disorder and argued for a scientific study of society to support a new social order. Durkheim examined how modern society could maintain solidarity amid individualism. Tocqueville considered democracy and the possibility of majority domination. Marx argued that political equality alone could not remove economic inequality and class exploitation. Their analyses helped make social change a central subject of sociology.

3. Intellectual forces: Renaissance and Enlightenment

The Renaissance, a revival of learning and arts in Europe from roughly the fourteenth to seventeenth centuries, encouraged humanism, individualism and curiosity about the world. Humanism emphasised human dignity, capacities and reason. Scholars increasingly studied society, history and culture through observation and secular inquiry rather than relying only on religious explanations. Scientific thinking, empirical evidence and critical examination gradually gained importance.

The Enlightenment of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries extended this confidence in reason. Thinkers questioned the authority of monarchy, Church and feudal tradition. They argued that institutions and customs could be examined critically and reformed. The period promoted scientific methods, individual rights, equality before the law and the belief that society could progress through knowledge and rational reform. Thinkers including Locke, Rousseau and Montesquieu contributed ideas about rights, popular sovereignty and political organisation.

These intellectual changes offered a method and a purpose for studying society: use reason, observation and evidence to explain social life, and investigate how institutions might be improved. Comte drew on the scientific outlook and proposed positivism, the study of society through systematic methods. The combination of intellectual inquiry with the practical challenge of rapid social transformation helped sociology develop as a distinct discipline.

Conclusion

Sociology emerged in response to the combined effects of industrialisation, urbanisation and new class relations; the political upheaval of the French Revolution; and intellectual movements that valued reason, science and critical inquiry. Together these forces made society appear open to systematic investigation and reform. Sociology developed as an effort to explain social order, inequality and change in the modern world.
