Module 1 · Introducing Sociology

What sociology studies

Sociology examines how people live together and how social relationships, institutions, rules, and shared meanings shape everyday life. It asks questions that go beyond an individual's personality: How do families, schools, workplaces, governments, and communities influence what people can do? How do people maintain or change those arrangements?

A useful starting point is to connect a personal experience with its wider setting. Suppose several students struggle to attend morning classes. A sociological explanation would ask about travel routes, work and care responsibilities, timetable design, and access to transport. It would not assume that every student's difficulty has the same cause.

Four classical contributions

Auguste Comte: positivism

Comte argued that society could be studied systematically. Positivism emphasizes careful observation and evidence when describing social patterns. A researcher might compare attendance records across semesters before explaining a change.

Émile Durkheim: social facts

Durkheim focused on social facts: ways of acting, thinking, or feeling that exist beyond one person and exert influence over people. Institutions and shared rules can shape individual choices. His concept of anomie describes weakened or unclear social regulation.

Karl Marx: class and conflict

Marx analyzed how ownership and control of productive resources divide social classes and create unequal interests. His approach asks who has power over resources, who performs the work, and how those relationships can produce conflict or change.

Max Weber: meaning and social action

Weber emphasized interpretive understanding, often called Verstehen: studying the meanings people attach to their actions. He also used ideal types as clear analytical models for comparing complex institutions, such as bureaucracy.

Two views of sociology’s scope

The formalistic school treats sociology as a specialized study of the forms of social relationships, such as cooperation, competition, and authority. The synthetic school sees sociology as a broader science that brings together insights about many areas of social life. The distinction is about the scope of the discipline, not about whether social life has one cause.

Worked example: unequal exam results

Imagine that exam results differ between groups of students. Start by describing the pattern accurately, then use a perspective to ask a focused question:

  • A Durkheimian question: How do school rules, shared expectations, and students’ connection to the institution affect study routines?
  • A Marxian question: Do students have unequal access to quiet study space, paid tutoring, devices, or time away from work?
  • A Weberian question: What meanings do students attach to the exam, and how do they understand the rules for success?

These are different analytical lenses. Evidence is still needed to determine which factors matter in a particular case.

A clear structure for a short answer

  1. Define the concept in one direct sentence.
  2. Name the thinker or school connected with it.
  3. Explain the idea in your own words.
  4. Apply it to a specific example and show what it helps explain.
  5. Close by stating one limit or a second perspective that could add another insight.

Check your understanding

How does a sociological explanation differ from an individual explanation?

It examines how relationships, institutions, rules, resources, and shared meanings shape a situation, while still recognizing that people make choices and may experience the same setting differently.

What does anomie mean in Durkheim’s work?

It refers to a condition in which social regulation or shared norms are weakened or unclear. Use the term in context and explain the social conditions involved.

How do the formalistic and synthetic schools differ?

The formalistic school focuses on distinct forms of social relationships; the synthetic school treats sociology as a broader field that integrates the study of social phenomena.

Module 2 · Practicing Sociology

Sociology becomes useful when a broad idea is turned into a clear question and examined with evidence. The sociological imagination, associated with C. Wright Mills, connects a person’s experience with the historical and institutional conditions around it. If many students struggle to attend an early class, for example, the inquiry can examine transport, paid work, care duties, timetable design, and access to study support as well as individual choices. A wider explanation should not erase personal agency; it helps identify the conditions within which choices are made.

Three analytical lenses

PerspectiveMain questionExample: a college rule
Structural functionalismHow does a social pattern relate to the operation or stability of a wider system?What intended functions does an attendance rule serve? Does it also create unintended effects, such as extra pressure on students with long commutes?
Conflict perspectiveWho has access to power and resources, and whose interests are served?Do students with fewer transport or technology options face a greater cost from the same rule?
Symbolic interactionismHow do people interpret situations and create shared meanings in everyday interaction?How do students and teachers define “participation,” and how do those definitions shape classroom behaviour?

Merton’s distinction helps refine a functional analysis: a manifest function is intended and recognized, while a latent function is unintended or less openly recognized. A consequence is not automatically beneficial just because it is called a function. Interactionist work often studies face-to-face meaning-making; conflict analysis brings inequality and power into focus. These perspectives guide questions; data are still needed to support a conclusion.

From a topic to a research plan

  1. Choose a bounded topic. “Student life” is too broad; “how first-year students describe barriers to arriving for an 8:30 class” is more workable.
  2. Review what is already known. Read course material and relevant studies so the question is informed and not a repetition of an unsupported assumption.
  3. Define the concepts. State what terms such as “late arrival,” “commute difficulty,” or “participation” mean for this particular inquiry.
  4. Select a method and sample. A questionnaire can describe a pattern across respondents; interviews can explore how people explain an experience. A sample should fit the question and the time available.
  5. Collect evidence ethically. Use a consistent procedure, record observations accurately, and protect participants’ privacy.
  6. Analyze before concluding. Look for patterns and exceptions. Compare evidence with the research question; do not treat an interesting example as proof about every student.
  7. Report the method and limits. Explain who participated, how information was gathered, what the findings can support, and what remains uncertain.

Quantitative approaches use measurements or counts to compare patterns; qualitative approaches examine meanings, experiences, and processes in depth. Surveys, interviews, observation, and document analysis can be combined when the question justifies it. Triangulation means comparing evidence from different methods or sources; agreement can strengthen an interpretation, while disagreement can reveal context that needs further study.

Ethics are part of the method

Explain the study in language participants can understand, make participation voluntary, and allow people to decline or stop without penalty. Collect only information needed for the question. Avoid names or identifying details in a report unless there is a clear, approved reason. Store notes securely, avoid misleading participants, and take special care where there is a power difference, sensitive subject, or risk of harm. Follow the university’s review and consent requirements before fieldwork begins.

Worked application: A class notices that students who live farther away arrive late more often. Attendance data can show whether a pattern exists, but by itself it may not explain why. Short voluntary interviews might identify transport changes, care duties, or timetable overlaps. The researcher should compare explanations carefully and avoid claiming that distance alone caused lateness.

Check your understanding

How does the sociological imagination connect a personal experience to society?

It asks how institutions, history, shared rules, and the distribution of resources shape an experience that might otherwise be treated only as an individual problem.

How are a manifest and a latent function different?

A manifest function is an intended, recognized consequence; a latent function is unintended or less recognized. Both need to be examined in context.

Why can a small convenience sample not represent all students?

People who are easiest to reach may differ from those not included. The researcher should describe how the sample was selected and limit claims to what the evidence supports.

Module 3 · Individual and Society

People develop identities and expectations through relationships. Sociology studies both the actions people take in particular situations and the patterns that make those situations recognizable. Interaction is shaped by setting, status, language, shared symbols, and the responses of other people; it is not simply a series of isolated personal decisions.

Groups and relationships

Primary and secondary groups

Cooley used primary group for close, enduring relationships involving face-to-face interaction, such as many family or friendship groups. A secondary group is generally more task-focused and organized around a specific purpose, such as a course committee. Real groups can contain features of both.

In-groups, out-groups, and reference groups

Sumner’s terms in-group and out-group describe a perceived “we” and “they”; the boundary may change with the situation. A reference group is one people use to evaluate themselves or guide aspirations, whether or not they belong to it.

Groups can offer belonging and mutual support, but their boundaries can also produce exclusion or pressure to conform. To study a group, ask who participates, what shared expectations operate, who can speak or make decisions, and how membership changes across settings.

Socialization across the life course

Socialization is the continuing process through which people learn and interpret social expectations, skills, identities, and ways of participating. Family, peers, schools, workplaces, religious communities, media, and online spaces may all contribute. They do not transmit one uniform message: expectations can differ and sometimes conflict.

  • Cooley’s looking-glass self: people form a sense of self partly by imagining how others see them and responding to those perceived judgments. This is about interpreted feedback, not a guarantee that the imagined judgment is accurate.
  • Mead’s account of the self: social interaction helps people learn to take the role of others. Mead described the “I” as the responding aspect of the self and the “Me” as the organized attitudes and expectations a person has learned from social life.
  • Resocialization: in some transitions, people learn new expectations and revise routines or identities. The degree of change depends on the setting and the person’s circumstances; it should not be assumed that everyone experiences it in the same way.

Goffman’s work on interaction draws attention to how people manage impressions in particular settings. A student may speak differently in a seminar, with friends, or in a job interview because each setting carries different expectations. The contrast is an analytical tool, not proof that one presentation is “real” and the others are false.

Social control and everyday example

Social control refers to processes that encourage or discourage behaviour in line with social expectations. Formal mechanisms include written rules and official sanctions; informal mechanisms include approval, criticism, humour, and peer pressure. Control can help coordinate shared activity, but it can also reinforce unequal expectations. Ask who sets the norm, how it is enforced, and whether its consequences differ across groups.

First-year college example: A new student learns the timetable, classroom routines, and ways to ask for help. Peers explain informal expectations that are missing from the handbook. The student may compare their progress with a reference group. A sociological account connects personal adjustment with interaction, group membership, institutional rules, and unequal access to support.

Check your understanding

What makes a group a reference group?

People use it as a point of comparison or a guide for evaluating themselves and their choices; membership is not required.

What is one difference between formal and informal social control?

Formal control uses organized rules and authorized sanctions; informal control works through everyday reactions such as approval, criticism, or peer expectations.

Why should socialization not be described as passive copying?

People interpret, negotiate, and sometimes resist expectations. Different agents may also offer conflicting messages, and people’s experiences vary by setting and position.

Module 4 · Sociology and Society

A society is not just a population living in one place. It involves patterned relationships, shared institutions, boundaries, and ways of organizing collective life. Sociologists examine how these arrangements meet needs, distribute resources, establish expectations, and change over time.

Institutions, status, and role

A social institution is an established pattern of norms and relationships organized around recurring areas of social life, such as education, family, economy, religion, or government. An institution is not the same thing as a single organization: education is an institution, while a particular college is an organization through which parts of education are arranged.

Status is a recognized social position; role is the set of expectations associated with a position. A person holds a status set, not only one status. An ascribed status is generally assigned rather than chosen, while an achieved status is gained or entered through action or circumstance. These labels need context; they do not mean every position is fully fixed or freely chosen. A master status is a position that becomes especially influential in how a person is identified in a particular setting.

  • Role set: the different relationships attached to one status. A teacher, for example, interacts with students, colleagues, administrators, and families.
  • Role conflict: incompatible demands from two or more statuses, such as a work shift clashing with a required class.
  • Role strain: competing demands within one status, such as a student balancing attendance, assignments, and exam preparation.

These terms describe pressures in social arrangements; they should not be used to blame an individual for conditions they cannot control. A good answer identifies the relevant expectations and explains the setting that produces the tension.

How social change can be analyzed

Social change is a sustained shift in relationships, institutions, norms, or patterns of collective life. Theories emphasize different drivers: technological change, economic conflict, population movement, political decisions, or transformations in shared values. No single factor explains every case, and a change can create benefits for some groups while imposing costs on others.

Ogburn’s idea of cultural lag draws attention to a possible difference in pace between material change and changes in norms, laws, or institutions. For example, communication technology may spread faster than school policies for using it. The term suggests a question to investigate; it does not mean that every slow rule is automatically wrong or that all social change follows the same sequence.

Worked application: smartphones in education. First describe the change: students and teachers now communicate through digital platforms. A functional question asks how the tools support coordination and what unintended consequences appear. A conflict question asks who has devices, data, quiet space, and control over platform rules. An interactionist question asks how “being available” or “participating” is understood online. A cultural-lag question asks whether institutional policies have kept pace with use. Evidence from different groups would be needed before drawing conclusions.

Check your understanding

How is an institution different from an organization?

An institution is a broad, established pattern of norms and relationships; an organization is a specific, structured group that may operate within an institution.

How do role conflict and role strain differ?

Role conflict concerns incompatible demands between statuses; role strain concerns competing demands within one status.

What does cultural lag help a researcher ask?

Whether material or technological change has moved faster than related norms, laws, or institutional arrangements, and what effects that timing difference has for particular groups.

Module 5 · Perceiving Social Dynamics: A Fieldwork Guide

Fieldwork gives students a structured way to observe or ask about social life in a defined setting. Its purpose is to practise sociological inquiry, not to claim that a small classroom project represents an entire community. Follow your instructor’s requirements and seek any required approval before contacting participants or collecting information.

Example inquiry: commuting and class arrival

Research question: “How do selected first-year students describe factors that affect their arrival for morning classes?” This wording asks about experiences without assuming that one factor causes lateness. A practical objective is to identify transport, timetable, work, care, and other factors participants say matter, then describe similarities and differences in their accounts.

  1. Set boundaries. Identify the class, time period, and group you can study responsibly. Define “morning class” and what counts as arrival for this project.
  2. Read and refine. Review relevant course concepts and existing material. Narrow the question if it is too broad, leading, sensitive, or difficult to answer with available time.
  3. Choose a design. A short questionnaire may summarize reported patterns. A few semi-structured interviews may explain how participants experience a commute. Observation may document a routine in a public setting, subject to consent and local rules.
  4. Plan the sample. State who can participate and how they will be invited. A small purposive or convenience sample can support an exploratory class exercise, but it cannot establish what all students experience. Do not present a sample as representative unless the design supports that claim.
  5. Pilot the questions. Check that wording is clear, neutral, and respectful. Revise questions that presume an answer, combine several issues, or ask for unnecessary personal details.
  6. Collect consistently. Use the same instructions and recording approach. Keep field notes factual; distinguish what was observed from what you inferred.
  7. Analyze and report limits. Summarize recurring themes or counts, consider exceptions, connect findings with concepts, and state what the sample and method cannot establish.

Sample neutral questions

  • “Can you describe your usual route to campus on a morning when you have an early class?”
  • “What factors, if any, make it easier or harder for you to arrive on time?”
  • “Have those factors changed during the semester? What changed?”
  • “Is there anything about the timetable or campus routine that you think should be considered?”

These prompts leave room for “none” or “not sure.” Avoid asking for a participant’s home address, exact live location, or other identifying information when broad categories will answer the research question.

Consent, privacy, and safe practice

Before an interview or questionnaire, explain the purpose, what participation involves, how notes will be used, who may see them, and that participation is voluntary. Ask for informed consent in the format required by the institution. Do not record audio or photographs without explicit permission and any required approval. Use pseudonyms or aggregated findings where possible, remove identifiers from notes, store records securely, and set a sensible deletion date. If a participant appears uncomfortable, pause or stop. Do not promise confidentiality that you cannot actually protect.

Make a modest claim from the evidence

For a small exploratory set of interviews, group similar responses into themes such as transport reliability or timetable overlap. For questionnaire counts, show the number of responses and how the question was phrased. Do not infer causation from a simple association. If evidence is mixed, report the difference instead of forcing a single story. A limitation is useful information: it tells the reader how far a finding can travel.

Suggested report structure

  1. Title and research question — name the setting and the bounded issue.
  2. Introduction and concepts — explain why the question matters sociologically and define key terms.
  3. Method — describe the sample, recruitment, questions or observation, consent, and analysis.
  4. Findings — present themes or counts accurately, using anonymized examples only when allowed.
  5. Discussion — relate findings to a course concept and consider another possible interpretation.
  6. Limits and conclusion — answer the question cautiously, identify what the study could not establish, and suggest a reasonable next question.
  7. References and appendix — list sources and include approved study materials, with no private participant details.

Check your understanding

Why should a fieldwork question avoid assuming that commuting causes lateness?

A leading question can distort responses, and the relationship may involve several factors. Neutral wording allows evidence to support, qualify, or reject the assumption.

What is a responsible conclusion from a small convenience sample?

Describe what the participating people reported and note the sample limits. Do not generalize to all students or claim causation without a design and evidence that support it.

What should a researcher do if a participant wants to stop?

Stop the participation without pressure, follow the consent plan for already collected material, and respect the participant’s privacy and institutional rules.
