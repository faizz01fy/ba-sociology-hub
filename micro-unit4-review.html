Unit 4: Shocking the Equilibrium

20-Minute Masterclass Study Video · Narrated by Kira (Friendly Voice Agent) · Demand Shocks, Supply Shocks, Avocado & Tomato Models (Figures 4.1 & 4.2), Hurricane Katrina Disruption, and MCQs 1 to 5.

Core Conceptual Notes & Exam Foundations

  • Equilibrium Inertia: Once a market reaches equilibrium ($Q_d = Q_s$), it persists indefinitely because neither buyers nor sellers have any pressure or incentive to alter the market clearing price.
  • Anatomy of a Shock: A shock is an unanticipated external event that shifts the demand curve, supply curve, or both, causing disequilibrium and driving market forces to establish a new equilibrium point ($e_1 \to e_2$).
  • Negative Demand Shock: Shifts the entire demand curve inward to the left (e.g., medical journal safety alert on prescription drugs), causing both equilibrium price and equilibrium quantity to drop.
  • Positive Demand Shock: Shifts demand outward to the right (e.g., nutritional studies praising fish creating a cross-market surge for complementary tartar sauce), causing both equilibrium price and quantity to increase.
  • Quantitative Demand Shift (Figure 4.1): A $\$55/\text{lb}$ surge in fresh tomato prices induces consumers to substitute avocados. Avocado demand shifts outward from $D_1$ to $D_2$. At the initial $\$2.00$ price, consumers demand 91 million lbs while suppliers offer only 80 million lbs. The resulting 11 million lb excess demand forces price upward to $\$2.20$ and expands equilibrium quantity to 83 million lbs.
  • Theoretical Distinction I: The outward shift of the avocado demand curve causes a movement along the unchanged upward-sloping supply curve.
  • Negative Supply Shock: Shifts supply inward to the left (e.g., Hurricane Katrina shutting down offshore Gulf oil rigs, refineries, and pipelines; metal input diversions to microchips), contracting output and driving market prices up sharply.
  • Positive Supply Shock: Shifts supply outward to the right (e.g., industrial robotics and automation leaps; discovery of thick low-sulfur coal seams in Wyoming's Powder River Basin), vastly increasing production capacity and reducing consumer prices.
  • Quantitative Supply Shift (Figure 4.2): A $\$0.55/\text{lb}$ increase in fertilizer input costs shifts the avocado supply curve leftward from $S_1$ to $S_2$. At $\$2.00$, growers only supply 69 million lbs against 80 million lbs demand, causing an 11 million lb shortage. Market pressures bid price up to $\$2.20$, while equilibrium quantity shrinks to 72 million lbs.
  • Theoretical Distinction II: The inward shift of the avocado supply curve causes a movement along the unchanged downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Multiple Choice Questions Mastery: All 5 examination MCQs from the text are verified:
    • Q1: Shock disruption $\to$ (c) Demand curve shifts, leading to a new equilibrium.
    • Q2: Positive demand shock $\to$ (c) Increase in quantity consumed at a higher price.
    • Q3: Negative supply shock $\to$ (c) Production disrupted, causing lower supply and higher prices.
    • Q4: Decreased consumer income $\to$ (c) Demand curve shifts inward, indicating decreased demand.
    • Q5: Technological efficiency advancement $\to$ (c) Supply curve shifts right, leading to lower prices.
💡 Critical Exam Tip: Never confuse a shift of a curve with a movement along a curve! A shift is caused by changes in non-price determinants (consumer income, tastes, related goods, input costs, technology). That shift alters market price, which subsequently causes a movement along the other curve until arriving at terminal equilibrium $e_2$.
