In English

Module 1 · Introducing Sociology

What sociology studies

Sociology examines how people live together and how social relationships, institutions, rules, and shared meanings shape everyday life. It asks questions that go beyond an individual's personality: How do families, schools, workplaces, governments, and communities influence what people can do? How do people maintain or change those arrangements?

A useful starting point is to connect a personal experience with its wider setting. Suppose several students struggle to attend morning classes. A sociological explanation would ask about travel routes, work and care responsibilities, timetable design, and access to transport. It would not assume that every student's difficulty has the same cause.

Four classical contributions

Auguste Comte: positivism

Comte argued that society could be studied systematically. Positivism emphasizes careful observation and evidence when describing social patterns. A researcher might compare attendance records across semesters before explaining a change.

Émile Durkheim: social facts

Durkheim focused on social facts: ways of acting, thinking, or feeling that exist beyond one person and exert influence over people. Institutions and shared rules can shape individual choices. His concept of anomie describes weakened or unclear social regulation.

Karl Marx: class and conflict

Marx analyzed how ownership and control of productive resources divide social classes and create unequal interests. His approach asks who has power over resources, who performs the work, and how those relationships can produce conflict or change.

Max Weber: meaning and social action

Weber emphasized interpretive understanding, often called Verstehen : studying the meanings people attach to their actions. He also used ideal types as clear analytical models for comparing complex institutions, such as bureaucracy.

Two views of sociology’s scope

The formalistic school treats sociology as a specialized study of the forms of social relationships, such as cooperation, competition, and authority. The synthetic school sees sociology as a broader science that brings together insights about many areas of social life. The distinction is about the scope of the discipline, not about whether social life has one cause.

Worked example: unequal exam results

Imagine that exam results differ between groups of students. Start by describing the pattern accurately, then use a perspective to ask a focused question:

A Durkheimian question: How do school rules, shared expectations, and students’ connection to the institution affect study routines?

A Marxian question: Do students have unequal access to quiet study space, paid tutoring, devices, or time away from work?

A Weberian question: What meanings do students attach to the exam, and how do they understand the rules for success?

These are different analytical lenses. Evidence is still needed to determine which factors matter in a particular case.

Module 2 · Practicing Sociology

Sociology becomes useful when a broad idea is turned into a clear question and examined with evidence. The sociological imagination , associated with C. Wright Mills, connects a person’s experience with the historical and institutional conditions around it. If many students struggle to attend an early class, for example, the inquiry can examine transport, paid work, care duties, timetable design, and access to study support as well as individual choices. A wider explanation should not erase personal agency; it helps identify the conditions within which choices are made.

Three analytical lenses

Merton’s distinction helps refine a functional analysis: a manifest function is intended and recognized, while a latent function is unintended or less openly recognized. A consequence is not automatically beneficial just because it is called a function. Interactionist work often studies face-to-face meaning-making; conflict analysis brings inequality and power into focus. These perspectives guide questions; data are still needed to support a conclusion.

From a topic to a research plan

Choose a bounded topic. “Student life” is too broad; “how first-year students describe barriers to arriving for an 8:30 class” is more workable.

Review what is already known. Read course material and relevant studies so the question is informed and not a repetition of an unsupported assumption.

Define the concepts. State what terms such as “late arrival,” “commute difficulty,” or “participation” mean for this particular inquiry.

Select a method and sample. A questionnaire can describe a pattern across respondents; interviews can explore how people explain an experience. A sample should fit the question and the time available.

Collect evidence ethically. Use a consistent procedure, record observations accurately, and protect participants’ privacy.

Analyze before concluding. Look for patterns and exceptions. Compare evidence with the research question; do not treat an interesting example as proof about every student.

Report the method and limits. Explain who participated, how information was gathered, what the findings can support, and what remains uncertain.

Quantitative approaches use measurements or counts to compare patterns; qualitative approaches examine meanings, experiences, and processes in depth. Surveys, interviews, observation, and document analysis can be combined when the question justifies it. Triangulation means comparing evidence from different methods or sources; agreement can strengthen an interpretation, while disagreement can reveal context that needs further study.

Ethics are part of the method

Explain the study in language participants can understand, make participation voluntary, and allow people to decline or stop without penalty. Collect only information needed for the question. Avoid names or identifying details in a report unless there is a clear, approved reason. Store notes securely, avoid misleading participants, and take special care where there is a power difference, sensitive subject, or risk of harm. Follow the university’s review and consent requirements before fieldwork begins.

Short voluntary interviews might identify transport changes, care duties, or timetable overlaps. The researcher should compare explanations carefully and avoid claiming that distance alone caused lateness.

Cognitive Dimensions of Bloom's Revised Taxonomy:

The syllabus integrates the hierarchical cognitive ladder:

1. Remember (R): Recall terms, thinkers, and definitions.

2. Understand (U): Comprehend paradigms, compare perspectives.

3. Apply (Ap): Use theories to diagnose live social situations.

4. Analyse (An): Dissect systemic causes of inequality and institutional lag.

5. Evaluate (E): Assess policies and ethical dilemmas.

6. Create (C): Formulate original qualitative research proposals.

ബ്ലൂംംസ് റിവൈൈ സ്ഡ് ടാക്സോണമിയുടെെ തലങ്ങൾ:

1. ഓർമ്മിിക്കുക (Remember - R): ചിന്തകരെെയും നിർവചനങ്ങളെെയും ഓർത്തെെ ടുക്കൽ.

2. മനസ്സിിലാക്കുക (Understand - U): സിദ്ധാന്തങ്ങളും ആശയങ്ങളും ഗ്രഹിക്കൽ.

3. പ്രയോഗിക്കുക (Apply - Ap): യഥാർത്ഥ ജീവിതത്തിൽ സിദ്ധാന്തങ്ങൾ പ്രാാവർത്തികമാക്കൽ.

5. വിലയിരുത്തുുക (Evaluate - E): നയങ്ങളെെയും സിദ്ധാന്തങ്ങളെെയും വിമർശനാത്മകമായി വിലയിരുത്തൽ.

6. സൃഷ്ടിക്കുക (Create - C): സ്വന്തമായി പുതിയ ഗവേേ ഷണ ആശയങ്ങൾ രൂപപ്പെെ ടുത്തൽ.

Four Knowledge Categories:

• Factual (F): Terminology, historical dates (e.g., 1838 Comte), specific names.

• Conceptual (C): Theories, typologies, schemas (Durkheim's solidarity, Marx's class conflict).

• Procedural (P): Methodological algorithms, research fieldwork steps, case study execution.

• Metacognitive (M): Reflexive awareness of one's own positionality, social biases, and analytical limitations.

UNIT 1: CONCEPT, DEFINITIONS & ESSENTIAL NATURE OF SOCIAL CONTROL

1. Concept and Historical Origin

Social control refers to the entire apparatus of social mechanisms, techniques, and strategies by which a society guides, regulates, and compels its members to conform to recognized values and norms. The concept was systematically introduced into sociological literature by Edward Alsworth Ross (E.A. Ross) in his groundbreaking 1901 book 'Social Control' .

2. Authoritative Sociological Definitions

E.A. Ross: "Social control is that system of social devices and mechanisms by which a society brings its members into conformity with accepted standards."

MacIver and Page: "Social control is the way in which entire social order coheres and maintains itself—how the whole system operates as a viable equilibrium."

Ogburn and Nimkoff: "The patterns of pressure which a society exerts to maintain order and established rules."

Calicut LMS Definition: "Social control relies on both internal and external methods to ensure social conformity, curb deviant behavior, and maintain stability."

3. Key Characteristics & Functions of Social Control

Universal & Omnipresent: Exists in every human society, from small hunter-gatherer bands to modern megacities.

Preserves Social Order: Prevents chaos, anomie, violence, and disintegration.

Internal & External Methods: Operates via internal conscience (guilt) as well as external sanctions (fines, jail).

Transmits Heritage: Ensures cultural traditions and legal codes pass uninterrupted across generations.

• 1. Sub-Groups of Various Types:

Macro-society is partitioned into smaller functional groupings that bind individuals into collective solidarity. Includes:

Primary Groups (family, peer playgroups), Secondary Groups (corporations, trade unions), Voluntary Associations (charity societies), and Compulsory Communities (municipal cities, territorial states).

• 2. Roles of Various Types:

Specialized division of functional duties. Society designates specific occupational, leadership, and parental expectations ensuring that all necessary tasks (food supply, law enforcement, healthcare) are performed systematically.

• 3. Regulative & Obligatory Norms:

Standards of conduct dividing societal action into mandatory duties and procedural restrictions, maintaining institutional order.

• 4. Cultural Values:

Ultimate criteria of moral desirability and ethical excellence that unite the population under a shared civilizational vision.
